    The Goodhart Plane

    A catalogue of failures and fixes: four Goodhart variants, five more failure shapes, four mitigations with their failure modes

    4 November 2026, coverage revision

    The idea in one paragraph

    A reward is a proxy. Choose a reference behavior or policy distribution and put its expected scores at the origin. Plot $\Delta R = R - R_0$ against $\Delta U = U - U_0$, where $U$ is the intended value. Optimization favors higher expected reward: rightward pressure, not a guarantee that every step moves right. A down-right arrow marks a transition that improves reward while worsening intended value; a flat rightward arrow gains reward without value. These patterns illustrate Goodhart failures, but do not identify their causes. The fifteen panels organize mechanisms and mitigations using this common visual language.

    How to read the figure.

    • Points versus arrows. A red-region point has higher reward and lower value than the chosen reference. A down-right arrow loses value relative to its own starting point, wherever it lies. For example, $(1,10) \to (2,9)$ is a harmful transition entirely in the upper-right quadrant. Existence of such a pair does not imply a red-region point for every reference.
    • Axes and reference. The schematic origin is a chosen comparison policy, not necessarily the first point of a path. B, G and N use the population means of their base distributions (zero before scaling); J uses the balanced policy; M uses the base policy. The measured example defines its own shared empirical reference. Dotted diagonal = equality after an explicitly chosen calibration, not a universal alignment test. $R=2U+7$ preserves rankings and can be rescaled onto it. Plot scales need not match across panels.
    • Colours. Red marks problematic selection or transitions. Green marks alternatives, constraints, and rescoring arrows; in K, L and O green arrows specifically change the scores of existing behaviors. Black arrows in K and M show policy responses. Grey is data or an evaluator's view. Hollow observer points in I and L depict estimated rather than true value and are not additional feasible outcomes.
    • Rows. Row 1: an axis key and the four variants of Manheim and Garrabrant [1]. Row 2: five additional mechanisms or patterns. Row 3: four mitigations (K, M, N, O), plus obfuscation (L), a failure mode of K.
    • Data and schematics. B, G and N use 300 simulated samples with fixed seeds; they are illustrations, not estimates of general performance. Their coordinates are scaled by 0.42. G and N clip out-of-range reward coordinates at the border, including the selected point and band mean; distances there are not meaningful. H and J use stated formulas. Other panel coordinates are schematic.
    • Limits of identification. Different mechanisms can produce the same picture. Section 2 suggests follow-up investigations and competing explanations; it does not supply sufficient diagnostic tests.
    • Prior art. Demski and Garrabrant [2] draw the four variants and quantilization on proxy-versus-value axes. Laidlaw et al. [27] show reference, hacking and regularized distributions on such axes. Gao et al. [4] plot proxy and gold rewards against optimization. This document contributes an expanded teaching synthesis, linking mechanisms to mitigation assumptions and one measured example. The wider historical comparisons, including Baker's effort-space geometry and Taylor and Russell's selection plots, are sourced in prior_planes_and_catalogues.md; the axes and multi-panel approach are not claimed as new.

    The panels

    The four Goodhart variants

    A. The plane [1, 2, 3]

    • Shape. Axes ΔR and ΔU from a reference policy, the calibrated-proxy diagonal, and two named quadrants. A move is a change in policy; a point is where that change lands.
    • Why. For finite MDPs under the policy-set assumptions of Skalse et al., nontrivial unhackable reward pairs must induce equivalent policy orderings. Constant-return rewards are an exception. Non-equivalence of nontrivial rewards therefore implies some disagreeing policy pair, not a red point relative to every reference. Existence does not imply that training finds that pair.

    B. Regressional [1, 4]

    • Shape. A cloud along the diagonal (300 points, U ~ N(0,1), R = U + N(0,1), seed 0; both axes scaled by 0.42). The top-R point lies to the right of, and below, the top-U point.
    • Why. Selecting on R also selects on R's error. With equal-variance Gaussian noise, E[U | R = r] = r/2: expected U still rises with the number of candidates searched, but only half as fast as R, and the gap to the oracle pick grows. In this independent Gaussian model, regret grows while expected utility improves. Other error models can produce falling utility, including bounded misspecification; heavy tails are one possible mechanism, not a necessary condition.

    C. Extremal [1, 5]

    • Shape. A curve on the diagonal inside the dashed box, bending down outside it. Schematic.
    • Why. R was fitted where data existed. Optimization moves the policy out of that region, where the fitted relation no longer holds. Pan et al. see sharp phase transitions as capability grows.

    D. Causal [1, 6]

    • Shape. A flat arrow to the right. Schematic.
    • Why. R rewards a correlate of U, not a cause. Length, keywords and confident tone correlate with quality in the training data; the agent raises them directly.

    E. Adversarial (cobra effect) [1, 7, 8]

    • Shape. A whole population shifts down and to the right. The arrows are drawn by other agents, not by our optimizer. Schematic.
    • Why. Once R pays, other agents produce what R counts (bred cobras, gamed credit scores). The measurement stays accurate; the measured thing changed.

    More failure shapes

    F. Tampering [9, 10]

    • Shape. A steep arrow down and to the right. Schematic; the same arrow can come from other mechanisms.
    • Why. The agent edits the measurement channel: the log, the tests, the reward function or its inputs. Denison et al. see this generalize from sycophancy to editing the reward code. Tampering can also move right with U unchanged, if the harm is only to the record.

    G. Heavy-tailed error [11, 12, 13]

    • Shape. A horizontal streak at U near 0 that runs to the right edge (300 points, U ~ N(0,1), R = U + Student-t error with 1.6 degrees of freedom, seed 1; points past the edge are drawn at the edge; both axes scaled by 0.42).
    • Why. Here independent Student-t error can dominate selection over Gaussian value at large n. Under their assumptions, Kwa et al. construct policies with arbitrarily high reward, little KL change and no utility gain. Their tested reward models had tails consistent with light-tailed error; this is a theoretical risk, not an empirical claim about all models.

    H. Overoptimization [4, 14, 15, 16]

    • Shape. One path with U rising to a peak and then falling while R keeps rising. Schematic: R(t) = -1.6 + 3.6t, U(t) = -0.6 + 4.4t - 4.8t², dots at equal steps of t.
    • Why. Reward-model error grows with distance from the base policy. Gao et al. fit scaling laws for the peak; Rafailov et al. find the same hump in direct alignment methods.

    I. Evaluator manipulation [17, 18, 10]

    • Shape. Two endpoints at the same R: a hollow point high on the diagonal (judged U) and a solid red point below (true U). The bracket is what the judge cannot see. Schematic.
    • Why. When R is a judgement, exploiting the judge can be cheaper than improving the outcome. Wen et al. show RLHF raising human approval while human error rates rise; sycophancy is the same move.

    J. Multitask under-investment [19, 20, 21]

    • Shape. An arc from balanced effort (green) to all effort on the measured task (red). Formula: effort a1 = 2cos θ on the measured task, a2 = 2sin θ on the unmeasured one; R = 0.85 a1, U = 0.8 (1.25 a2 + 0.3 a1 - 0.6 - 0.9 e^(-3 a2)). Subtract R and U of the utility-maximizing point on the sampled arc; plot the utility difference at scale 0.8. The balanced policy is the reference.
    • Why. Effort is finite. Paying for the measured attribute pulls effort from the unmeasured ones. Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell: with shared resources, an incomplete proxy can drive U arbitrarily low.

    Mitigations and their failure modes

    K. A learning grader [22]

    • Shape. Two stages. Green arrows (1): the same behaviours rescored under R_new; they lose R, the harm is unchanged. Black arrow (2): the agent's response to R_new, which is where U changes. Schematic.
    • How it works. Random audits reveal the truth on a few samples. The grader retrains on them and closes each blind spot the agent finds. Rudoler et al.: error and audit rate both vanish iff the grader class has finite Littlestone dimension (realizable case). Stage 2 happens only if the rescoring is large and frequent enough to change what pays; detection alone does not move the agent.

    L. Obfuscation [23, 24, 25]

    • Shape. Stage 1 as in K. In stage 2 one hack stays in the red; only its ghost on the diagonal, what the monitor sees, has moved. Schematic.
    • Why. This is K's failure mode, not a mitigation. A monitor inside the reward is itself a proxy. Baker et al. see obfuscated reward hacking; Cundy and Gleave get honesty or evasion depending on detector accuracy, KL and exploration; Taufeeque et al. map where each outcome appears.

    M. Leash (KL penalty) [26, 27, 11]

    • Shape. A dashed region around the base policy at the origin. The black arrow stops where the penalty bites; the red continuation is marked costly. A dotted streak escapes along the bottom. Schematic: the set of policies within a KL budget is not a disc in (R, U), and a penalty is a slope, not a wall.
    • How it works. A KL or occupancy penalty keeps the policy near the region where R was fitted. Laidlaw et al. show that regularizing the state-action occupancy, not the action distribution, is the right quantity for correlated proxies. Nearness to the base policy does not by itself keep the policy inside the reward model's reliable region.

    N. Quantilization [28, 29]

    • Shape. The heavy-tailed cloud of G. A vertical line marks the top-10% band by R; the star is the band's mean U (0.24); the red point is the argmax-R sample (U = 0.01). This single realization illustrates the mechanism, not an estimate of average performance.
    • How it works. Sample from the base distribution conditioned on the top fraction q by reward, randomizing ties. For any nonnegative cost with finite base expectation, the expected cost is at most 1/q times that expectation: the probability ratio is bounded pointwise. This is a relative bound, not an absolute safety guarantee. In the independent Gaussian model of B, maximization has higher expected U. Heavy tails are not necessary for quantilization to help: suppose 99% of base outcomes have (R,U)=(1,1) and 1% have (2,0). Best-of-n has expected U=0.99^n, while a top-10% quantilizer has expected U=0.9 (ties randomized). All errors are bounded.

    O. Pessimistic ensemble [30, 31, 32]

    • Shape. Horizontal bars show how much the reward models disagree. Green arrows rescore each hack to the low end of its bar (stage 1 only; the agent's response is not drawn). One red point with a short bar keeps its R. Schematic.
    • How it works. Reward models may disagree on unfamiliar inputs, including some hacks; shared errors can remain confident. Scoring by the minimum or by an uncertainty penalty removes R from those points (Coste et al.).

    Possible explanations and follow-up investigations

    Failure panels B–J and L. A signature is consistent with its row's mechanism and usually with others; the third column suggests follow-up investigations, not sufficient tests for identification. Compare competing explanations before choosing a response.

    Panel Signature on the plane Follow-up investigation Mitigation
    B. Regressional A cloud along the diagonal (300 points, U ~ N(0,1), R = U + N(0,1), seed 0; both axes scaled by 0.42). The top-R point lies to the right of, and below, the top-U point. Compare held-out U of selected and oracle samples across n and repeated draws. Estimate conditional error and compare alternative models: growing regret alone identifies neither Gaussian noise nor a unique mechanism. Reduce the error variance of R (a better reward model, ensembles, O). Searching less helps only if the lost R was error, which this model does not guarantee.
    C. Extremal A curve on the diagonal inside the dashed box, bending down outside it Track distance from the training distribution (KL to the base policy, an out-of-distribution score) next to gold U. A coincident break motivates investigating distribution shift. KL and OOD scores can miss relevant shifts; compare controlled in-distribution and shifted evaluations. Leash (M); early stopping (H); ensembles disagree out of distribution (O).
    D. Causal A flat arrow to the right Intervene: change the suspected correlate while holding content fixed (pad length, add keywords). R up and U unchanged under the intervention is causal Goodhart on that feature; a flat arrow without the intervention is not evidence. Remove or control the feature in R; reward causes, which needs a causal model; a learning grader that relabels (K).
    E. Adversarial (cobra effect) A whole population shifts down and to the right. The arrows are drawn by other agents, not by our optimizer Compare incentive and control groups over time, with measurement held fixed. Check action traces for strategic responses; a before/after population shift alone can reflect unrelated changes. Commit to robust thresholds; reward improvement rather than gaming; relative or yardstick measures; the honesty-games catalogue.
    F. Tampering A steep arrow down and to the right Keep a hidden, environment-owned record and compare it with the visible one on random audits. Divergence warrants checking instrumentation and action traces; attribute tampering only after excluding logging faults and other causes. Environment-owned logs; optimize the current reward function, not the future one; decoupled approval; audits with a penalty plus a learning grader (K).
    G. Heavy-tailed error A horizontal streak at U near 0 that runs to the right edge (300 points, U ~ N(0,1), R = U + Student-t error with 1.6 degrees of freedom, seed 1; points past the edge are drawn at the edge; both axes scaled by 0.42). Audit high-score samples as well as a representative sample, accounting for sampling probabilities. Compare tail models and thresholds with uncertainty. Flat U can reflect saturation or bounded misspecification; finite samples cannot establish asymptotic tails. Student-t(1.6) has no finite variance or population kurtosis. Quantilization (N); bounded or clipped rewards limit score magnitude, not harm; ensembles (O) require validated error coverage under selection, not just member diversity.
    H. Overoptimization One path with U rising to a peak and then falling while R keeps rising Evaluate gold U at checkpoints against KL from the base policy. A reproducible hump indicates overoptimization relative to that evaluation signal. Select stopping checkpoints on validation data and confirm on fresh evaluations. Early stopping (Karwowski et al. derive a rule); leash (M); a larger reward model or more data; online reward-model updates (K).
    I. Evaluator manipulation Two endpoints at the same R: a hollow point high on the diagonal (judged U) and a solid red point below (true U). The bracket is what the judge cannot see On fixed verifiable tasks, track judge errors, then intervene on suspected persuasive features while preserving correctness. A rising false-positive rate alone can reflect ordinary evaluator error; controlled interventions and action traces strengthen a manipulation account. Give the judge an adversary (debate, critics); verifiable rewards where possible; a learning grader trained on audits (K).
    J. Multitask under-investment An arc from balanced effort (green) to all effort on the measured task (red). Measure omitted attributes and effort allocation separately. Vary incentives under a controlled resource budget to distinguish resource diversion from changes in difficulty or capability. Weaker incentives or fixed pay on the measured task; add attributes to R; update the proxy interactively as the agent changes.
    L. Obfuscation Stage 1 as in K. In stage 2 one hack stays in the red; only its ghost on the diagonal, what the monitor sees, has moved On random audits, compare the monitor's flag rate with the hack rate in a hidden record. A hidden-hack rate that rises while the flag rate falls is the signature. Expect it under on-policy RL with much exploration and weak KL against a monitor in the reward; a falling flag rate alone can also mean fewer hacks. Keep the monitor out of the training reward, or use a strong KL and a high-accuracy detector; paraphrase to break hidden channels; random audits of the hidden record.

    Mitigations and their failure modes

    A complement must address the matching failure condition under stated assumptions. These are conditional candidates, not guarantees; methods sharing the same failure condition do not establish coverage. Obfuscation (L) is a failure of K and is listed with the failures above.

    Panel Fails when Conditional complements
    K. A learning grader The hack is invisible in the grader's inputs (L); the truth is not a function of those inputs (evidence erased); or audits are too rare for the penalty to bite, so stage 1 happens and stage 2 does not sufficiently reduce a particular harmful behavior. Hack invisible in the inputs → acquire independently verifiable evidence; a sealed confession preserves an account but does not establish its truth. Evidence erased → protected environment-owned records, assuming the agent cannot alter or bypass them. Audits too rare → increase effective detection and enforceable penalties; in a one-shot score model deterrence requires p × detection probability × penalty > hack gain. If detection is zero or penalties are too small, audit frequency alone cannot suffice.
    M. Leash (KL penalty) Under the assumptions in G, a policy can place enough mass on extreme errors to defeat a KL guarantee. KL constrains event probabilities but does not impose a uniform probability-amplification bound; closeness alone is not safety. Mass on extreme errors → quantilization (N) bounds probability amplification, provided base expected harm is finite and acceptably small for the chosen q. Ensembles (O) and lower optimization strength are candidates to test, not guarantees: even independent members can share errors on selected inputs. See the coverage companion, example C8.
    N. Quantilization The base distribution has excessive expected harm; q is too small for the bound to be useful; or reward selection is already reliable and the constraint sacrifices useful optimization. Harmful base distribution → a safer base policy; audit base samples to estimate the base harm that the 1/q bound multiplies. q too small → choose q from the audited base harm and the harm budget (expected harm ≤ base harm / q). Reward already reliable → plain optimization where audits show that. The leash (M) does not cover these conditions: it is anchored to the same base distribution.
    O. Pessimistic ensemble All members share pretraining or data, so they share errors. Eisenstein et al.: ensembles mitigate but do not eliminate hacking. Shared errors → seek different evidence sources and validate joint errors on selected candidates; different pretraining, data or architecture does not establish independence. Audits (K) help only when they observe the missed harm with sufficient coverage and enforcement. Weight averaging (WARM) does not by itself establish coverage of shared blind spots.

    Using the plane on a real system

    Choose an intended-value measurement that is independent of the optimization signal: a held-out gold model (as in Gao et al. [4]), verifiable outcomes, human audit labels, or an environment-owned hidden record. A held-out evaluator is still a proxy and can share errors with the training evaluator. Report uncertainty and disagreement across independent measures where possible. Random audits estimate population rates; targeted audits can find rare failures, but require sampling corrections for population estimates.

    Keep a common evaluation rule and reference when comparing policies. Plot sample clouds separately from policy or checkpoint means: these are different units of observation. When the training reward changes, show its history separately from a fixed evaluation score. Choose stopping points on validation data and confirm them on fresh evaluations, rather than repeatedly optimizing the same nominally held-out signal.

    Measured case: the confession testbed

    The testbed contains a forbidden shortcut, a log-erasing tile and a confession channel. A grader sees the confession and visible log; random audits reveal the hidden record. Window statistics are recorded during training.

    Three quantities, kept separate:

    • $R_{train}$ is the realized movement reward under the current training grader and reward scheme. In the coupled scheme it depends on the grader's probability score; it changes as the grader learns. In the sealed scheme movement receives task reward, while confession receives a separate honesty reward. There is no single plotted scalar that represents both sealed objectives. The movement update averages over possible confessions; the recorded realized reward is a sample from that same conditional expectation.
    • $R_{eval}$ is a common, frozen task-only scoring rule: goal reward minus step cost. It is evaluated on every recorded episode in every condition. It deliberately ignores rule-breaking, lying and erasure, so it is a comparable but incomplete proxy; it is not a frozen learned grader.
    • $U = R_{eval} - [\text{forbidden tile}] - [\text{lie}] - [\text{log erased}]$. All weights are 1, a stated choice. The components are recorded separately; other weights change the interpretation.

    Run and reference. Layout erase_pays, features full, $p=0.02$, lie penalty $L=1.0$; 15,000 training episodes in 10 windows, 10 seeds per condition. Each point averages a 1,500-episode training window, not evaluation of one frozen checkpoint. The reference is the first window of the fixed-grader/coupled condition, paired by seed and shared by all conditions. It is not the initial policy. Error bars are approximate, pointwise 95% Student-t intervals over seeds (9 degrees of freedom), including paired reference subtraction on the delta plot. They are not simultaneous trajectory bands. The JSON retains per-seed windows and configuration metadata.

    What these measurements support:

    • Common-score comparisons. The left plot uses the same $R_{eval}$ and reference for all conditions. Endpoint $U$ differs mainly with erasure (fixed coupled 0.70, co-trained coupled 0.20, sealed 0.00). Compare that plot to assess changes in task score and intended value; do not interpret differences in $R_{train}$ between conditions as moves under one fixed reward.
    • Persistent lying, not unchanged total harm. Final lie rates remain 0.99 in co-trained coupled and 0.99 in sealed training at this low audit rate. Erasure and aggregate U do change. These observations show incomplete deterrence of lying, not an absence of every behavioral response. A low thresholded miss rate from an earlier sweep is not a measurement of the probability scores used here and cannot establish how strongly lies were rescored.
    • Fluctuations are descriptive. In co-trained coupled runs, mean erasure rises from 0.17 in window 6 to 0.44 in window 8, then falls to 0.20. This is consistent with adaptation, but window averages do not establish an arms race. That would require grader-score histories, refit events, and controlled comparisons of policies under frozen graders.
    • Seed variation. The right plot shows actual final-window U by seed. Wide mean intervals alone would not establish distinct outcome modes. The saved seed records allow examining the corresponding erasure rates and computing paired comparisons without inferring them from marginal error bars.
    • Cover-up versus honesty. The seal nearly eliminates erasure, but lying remains frequent. The original sweep's cover-up statistic is the conjunction of hacking, erasing and hiding. A reduction in that statistic does not establish honesty. Here the improvement in U is mainly evidence preservation, not truthful confession. Visible evidence and true utility remain distinct quantities.
    • Final hack rates round to 1.00 in all conditions. These runs do not demonstrate deterrence of the shortcut. Higher audit rates belong to other cells of the earlier sweep and are not tested here.

    Limits of the picture

    • Scalar value and reference choice. U hides tradeoffs, and quadrant membership depends on the reference. Report components, weights and the reference alongside the aggregate. Harmful transitions need not end in the red quadrant.
    • Observation is not truth. I and L overlay an observer's estimate on the true-value axis as a visual device. The measured example's task score and utility are deliberately different; neither is a generic solution to measuring real-world intent.
    • Mechanisms are not identified by shape. Several mechanisms share a picture, and several patterns can occur together. Controlled investigations are needed beyond the diagram.
    • Rescoring is not behavior change. K and O replace R. For a reference-centered plot, the reference must also be rescored (the schematics hold its score fixed). Only the subsequent response changes outcomes. Training-reward histories with changing graders cannot be read as fixed-reward policy trajectories.
    • Illustrations versus measurements. B, G and N are simulated; H and J follow formulas; other panels are schematic. The measured case uses only one parameter cell and ten seeds per condition. No universal effectiveness ranking follows from it.
    • Optimization stage. Label trajectories by gradient steps, candidates searched or evaluation rounds. Inference-time selection applies optimization pressure even with a frozen evaluator.
    • Projection and scope. A–O are selected, overlapping examples, not an exhaustive taxonomy. Equal expected scores can hide different tail risks, subgroup outcomes and temporal effects. Correct rewards can coexist with goal misgeneralization. Restrictions change which actions are reachable, not necessarily their scores. The coverage companion records these gaps, a literature crosswalk and eight exact constructed checks; it does not claim saturation.
    • Not a predictive theory. The picture organizes results and assumptions; it does not make every rightward move harmful or establish that optimization will find every available exploit.
