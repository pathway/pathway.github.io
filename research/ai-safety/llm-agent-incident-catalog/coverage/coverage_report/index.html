    Coverage of specification-based safety over real LLM-agent incidents

    Question: for real LLM-agent incidents, what fraction could a safety constraint written BEFORE deployment (a cost function, rule or shield of the kind used in constrained MDPs [1][2]) have blocked? This is the empirical analogue of Breiman's point that goodness-of-fit tests lose power in high dimensions [3]: the claim under test is "specification-based safety has little coverage of real agent failures".

    Inter-rater agreement on writable_in_advance

    Two raters: claude-sonnet-5-5 (main) and claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 (second), same prompt, independent calls.

    Set N raw agreement Cohen's kappa [10]
    all stage-1 agentic rows, 3 classes 140 0.52 0.24
    main analysis rows, 3 classes 103 0.56 0.24
    main analysis rows, binary (yes+partial vs no) 103 0.85 0.07

    The yes/partial boundary is where the raters disagree most; Haiku calls yes more often than Sonnet. The binary kappa is low despite high raw agreement because Haiku almost never answers no (3/103 vs Sonnet 14/103): with one rater's marginal that skewed, chance agreement is already high (kappa prevalence effect). The no fraction is therefore rater-dependent and the Sonnet value should be read as the upper of the two estimates.

    Cost

    151 claude CLI calls in total (stage 1 batches of 12, stage 2 batches of 6, two stage-2 raters); logged cost USD 6.87. All responses cached under $SCRATCH/cache (a local scratch directory).

    Limitations

    • Selection bias. AIID collects incidents that were newsworthy enough to be reported [4]. Quiet failures caught by existing guardrails never enter the database, so the denominator is "harms that escaped and were reported", which biases the yes fraction DOWN (blockable harms that were blocked are invisible) and biases toward dramatic, irreversible, well-documented cases, which biases yes UP. The direction of net bias is unknown.
    • Model-judged labels. Both raters are LLMs. Kappa measures consistency between two models, not truth. The 15-item sample exists so a human can estimate the error rate; until then the fractions carry an unmeasured label-error term on top of the Wilson interval.
    • Hindsight bias. Knowing the outcome makes any rule look obvious. The prompt required the rule to be (a) generic to the deployment class, (b) checkable on observable action features without judging intent, (c) plausible in a standard pre-deployment spec, (d) compatible with the task. This reduces but does not remove the bias; a yes means "a rule of this form exists", not "a designer would have written it".
    • Prefilter recall. The regex prefilter removed 633 of 1282 incidents before any model saw them. Agentic incidents described without any keyword are lost.
    • Thin records. Each incident was judged from title, AIID description and the first 1,500 characters of its first report. Rules depend on details that may be absent.
    • Supplementary rows are not deployed-system incidents. The Agents of Chaos cases are from a red-team lab deployment [5]; Greshake et al. is a research demonstration [7]. They are labelled by source and can be dropped from the main table (AIID-only N=97).

    What the numbers show and do not show

    They show, for reported LLM-agent incidents since 2023 as judged by two LLM raters, the share whose first-order harm has the shape of a mechanically checkable constraint on action features (yes), the share where only part of the harm has that shape (partial), and the share where the harm is defined by context, intent or honesty and no such constraint exists (no). Two models of different size agree only weakly on the three-way split (kappa 0.24); the yes/partial boundary is unstable and the three-way fractions should be read as approximate. On the binary split (blockable at all vs not) raw agreement is 0.85 but kappa is 0.07 because Haiku rarely says no; the no share is between 0.03 and 0.14 depending on rater.

    They do not show that the yes rules would have been written, deployed, or kept switched on; they do not show that a rule has acceptable false-positive cost; they do not measure incidents that never became news; and they do not validate the labels against human judgement. A high yes share refutes the strong claim "specification-based safety has little coverage"; it does not establish that specification is sufficient, because partial and no cases are exactly the deception, injection and intent failures that a specification cannot express.
