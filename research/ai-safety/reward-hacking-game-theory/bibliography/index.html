    [1] Glenn W. Brier. "Verification of Forecasts Expressed in Terms of Probability." Monthly Weather Review 78(1):1-3, 1950. <https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493(1950)078<0001:VOFEIT>2.0.CO;2>

    • Game model: Single forecaster, quadratic scoring rule
    • Link to reward hacking: First strictly proper scoring rule: a forecaster who is paid by the Brier score maximises expected reward only by reporting true beliefs, the base unit of all calibration rewards for AI.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Brier proposes a verification score for probabilistic weather forecasts: the mean squared difference between forecast probabilities and the observed outcome indicators. He notes that a forecaster minimises the expected score by stating his true probabilities, so the score cannot be improved by hedging. This quadratic score is now called the Brier score. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [2] William Vickrey. "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders." Journal of Finance 16(1):8-37, 1961. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1961.tb02789.x

    • Game model: Second-price auction
    • Link to reward hacking: First dominant-strategy truthful mechanism: an agent's report sets the outcome but not its own price, so lying cannot help.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Vickrey analyzes auctions and marketing schemes under strategic bidding. He shows that in a second-price sealed-bid auction each bidder's dominant strategy is to bid its true value, and compares the revenue of auction formats. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [3] Gary S. Becker. "Crime and Punishment: An Economic Approach." Journal of Political Economy 76(2): 169-217, 1968. https://doi.org/10.1086/259394

    • Game model: rational-choice deterrence (enforcer vs offender)
    • Link to reward hacking: Foundational deterrence model of detection probability times penalty, used to reason about penalizing and detecting reward hacking under imperfect monitoring.
    • Area: Principal–agent, contracts and incentive analysis

    The paper models crime as a rational choice in which offenders compare expected gains with the probability and severity of punishment. It derives the socially optimal levels of enforcement expenditure and penalties that minimize social loss from crime and enforcement. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [4] Leonard J. Savage. "Elicitation of Personal Probabilities and Expectations." Journal of the American Statistical Association 66(336):783-801, 1971. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1971.10482346

    • Game model: Single agent, characterisation of proper scoring rules
    • Link to reward hacking: Characterises the full class of proper scoring rules (Bregman/convex-function form), giving designers a menu of honest-reporting rewards for eliciting probabilities and expectations from AI models.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Savage develops a general theory of proper scoring rules for eliciting a person's subjective probabilities and expectations. He characterises the scoring rules under which a person who maximises expected score reports their true probability or expectation, and discusses practical issues of eliciting personal probabilities. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [5] Theodore Groves. "Incentives in Teams." Econometrica 41(4):617-631, 1973. https://doi.org/10.2307/1914085

    • Game model: Dominant-strategy mechanism with transfers
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows how to align each agent's reward with total welfare so honest reporting is dominant; a template for reward design in multi-agent AI.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Groves studies a team in which members have private information and their own objectives. He constructs transfer schemes under which each member's payoff equals the team's total payoff plus a term it cannot affect, so truthful reporting is a dominant strategy (the Groves, later VCG, mechanisms). (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [6] Steven Kerr. "On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B." Academy of Management Journal 18(4): 769-783, 1975. https://doi.org/10.2307/255378

    • Game model: informal principal-agent / incentive misalignment
    • Link to reward hacking: Classic statement that agents optimize the rewarded measure rather than the intended goal, the organizational analogue of specification gaming.
    • Area: Principal–agent, contracts and incentive analysis

    The article documents many organizations (politics, medicine, universities, business) whose formal reward systems pay for behaviour A while the organization hopes for a different behaviour B. It argues that people do what is rewarded, so such reward systems produce the unwanted behaviour. It identifies causes such as a fixation on objective, measurable criteria. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [7] Robert E. Lucas. "Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique." Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy 1: 19-46, 1976. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2231(76)80003-6

    • Game model: rational-expectations policy game (agents respond to the rule)
    • Link to reward hacking: Explains why a proxy relation learned before optimization breaks once an optimizer acts on it, the same mechanism as reward-model overoptimization.
    • Area: Principal–agent, contracts and incentive analysis

    The paper argues that econometric relations estimated under one policy regime are not stable when policy changes, because agents' decision rules depend on the policy. Policy evaluation with fixed estimated parameters is therefore invalid; models must be built from structural parameters of agents' optimization problems. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [8] Donald T. Campbell. "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change." Evaluation and Program Planning 2(1): 67-90, 1979. https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X

    • Game model: informal; indicator corruption under strategic pressure
    • Link to reward hacking: Campbell's Law is the social-science counterpart of Goodhart's Law and is cited alongside it for metric and reward gaming.
    • Area: Principal–agent, contracts and incentive analysis

    The paper discusses methods for evaluating social programs and their political context. It states what is now called Campbell's Law: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the processes it is meant to monitor. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [9] Roger B. Myerson. "Incentive Compatibility and the Bargaining Problem." Econometrica 47(1):61-73, 1979. https://doi.org/10.2307/1912346

    • Game model: Bayesian mechanism design
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows it is without loss to look for reward schemes under which truthful reports are an equilibrium.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Myerson studies bargaining and collective choice under incomplete information. He states the revelation principle: any outcome achievable by some mechanism in Bayesian equilibrium can be achieved by an incentive-compatible direct mechanism in which agents report their types truthfully. He uses it to characterize efficient bargaining solutions. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [10] Paul R. Milgrom. "Good News and Bad News: Representation Theorems and Applications." Bell Journal of Economics 12(2):380-391, 1981. https://doi.org/10.2307/3003562

    • Game model: Persuasion game with verifiable messages
    • Link to reward hacking: Proves the unraveling result: withheld information is read as bad news, so truthful disclosure is the equilibrium.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Milgrom defines when news is 'favorable' (monotone likelihood ratio) and gives representation theorems. Applications include that buyers expect any product information withheld by a salesman to be unfavourable to his product, which yields full disclosure in a persuasion game. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [11] Ashok K. Chandra, Dexter C. Kozen, Larry J. Stockmeyer. "Alternation." Journal of the ACM 28(1), 1981. https://doi.org/10.1145/322234.322243

    • Game model: Existential vs universal player game
    • Link to reward hacking: Alternating polynomial time = PSPACE is the result Irving et al. use to argue debate with an optimal honest player reaches PSPACE.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    The paper defines alternating Turing machines, which alternate between existential and universal states, equivalent to a two-player game between a player who claims acceptance and one who denies it. It proves that alternating polynomial time equals PSPACE, and relates alternating time and space to deterministic classes. This gives the game view of computation that debate uses. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [12] Edward P. Lazear, Sherwin Rosen. "Rank-Order Tournaments as Optimum Labor Contracts." Journal of Political Economy 89(5):841-864, 1981. https://doi.org/10.1086/261010

    • Game model: Rank-order tournament
    • Link to reward hacking: Rank-based rewards filter common noise; relevant to comparative AI evaluation, but they invite sabotage of rivals.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Lazear and Rosen show that pay based on rank can give the same efficient effort as pay based on output when workers are risk neutral, and that risk-averse workers may prefer it. They discuss adverse selection with heterogeneous ability and handicapping when abilities are observable. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [13] Sanford J. Grossman. "The Informational Role of Warranties and Private Disclosure about Product Quality." Journal of Law and Economics 24(3):461-483, 1981. https://doi.org/10.1086/466995

    • Game model: Verifiable disclosure; skeptical buyer
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows that skeptical inference from silence makes full voluntary disclosure an equilibrium, so an AI overseer that penalizes missing evidence gets disclosure for free.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Grossman studies a seller who knows product quality and can make verifiable claims or offer warranties. He shows that, when disclosure is costless and verifiable, buyers infer that undisclosed quality is low, so the seller discloses fully. Mandatory disclosure is then unnecessary. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [14] David M. Kreps, Robert Wilson. "Reputation and Imperfect Information." Journal of Economic Theory 27(2):253-279, 1982. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0531(82)90030-8

    • Game model: Finitely repeated game with incomplete information
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows how a small chance of an honest type sustains honest behaviour by strategic players, and why behaviour can change near the end; relevant to track records and treacherous turns in AI.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Kreps and Wilson study the chain-store game with a small prior probability that the incumbent is a 'tough' type. The incumbent builds a reputation by imitating the tough type for most of the game, which resolves Selten's chain-store paradox. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [15] Vincent P. Crawford, Joel Sobel. "Strategic Information Transmission." Econometrica 50(6):1431-1451, 1982. https://doi.org/10.2307/1913390

    • Game model: Cheap talk, one sender
    • Link to reward hacking: Sets the limit on honesty without verification: a misaligned AI advisor reveals only coarse information, less as misalignment grows.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    An informed sender with a bias sends a costless message to a receiver who then acts. All equilibria are partition equilibria: the sender reveals only which interval the state lies in, and the number of intervals falls as the bias grows. Communication is more informative when interests are closer. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [16] Andrew G. Barto, Richard S. Sutton, Charles W. Anderson. "Neuronlike adaptive elements that can solve difficult learning control problems." IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, SMC-13(5):834-846, 1983. https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMC.1983.6313077

    • Game model: internal critic generates reinforcement for actor
    • Link to reward hacking: The critic turns env reward into an internal score for the actor, the first internal grader in RL; it supports honest behaviour only while the critic stays grounded in env reward, and the actor can exploit critic errors.
    • Area: Decomposed agents (internal games between parts)

    The paper introduces an architecture of two neuron-like elements for the cart-pole balancing task: an associative search element (ASE) that chooses actions and an adaptive critic element (ACE) that learns to predict reinforcement and sends an improved internal reinforcement signal to the ASE. This is the origin of the actor-critic split in reinforcement learning. The critic converts sparse failure signals into a denser evaluation that trains the actor. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [17] C. A. E. Goodhart. "Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience." In Monetary Theory and Practice, Macmillan (orig. 1975, Papers in Monetary Economics, Reserve Bank of Australia), 1984. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-349-17295-5_4

    • Game model: informal; strategic response to a control target
    • Link to reward hacking: Origin of Goodhart's Law, the central concept that AI-safety papers use to describe proxy-reward over-optimization.
    • Area: Principal–agent, contracts and incentive analysis

    The chapter reviews UK monetary policy in the early 1970s and the use of monetary aggregates as policy targets. It contains the observation later called Goodhart's Law: any observed statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. The 1984 book version reprints the 1975 paper. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [18] Andrei Shleifer. "A Theory of Yardstick Competition." RAND Journal of Economics 16(3):319-327, 1985. https://doi.org/10.2307/2555560

    • Game model: Relative-performance regulation
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows that paying an agent based on peers' reports removes its incentive to lie about its own; relevant to benchmarking AI agents against each other.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    In the typical regulatory scheme a franchised monopoly has little incentive to reduce costs. Shleifer proposes that the price a regulated firm receives depend on the costs of identical firms. This yardstick competition gives efficient cost reduction and removes the incentive to misreport costs; it extends to heterogeneous firms with observable differences. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [19] László Babai. "Trading group theory for randomness." STOC 1985, 1985. https://doi.org/10.1145/22145.22192

    • Game model: Arthur-Merlin game with public coins
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows a weak verifier can extract truth with public random challenges alone, so an overseer need not hide its questions, only make them unpredictable.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Babai introduces Arthur-Merlin games, interactive proofs in which the verifier (Arthur) uses public random coins and the all-powerful prover (Merlin) answers. He defines the hierarchy of Arthur-Merlin classes and uses them to place matrix group problems in low complexity classes. The paper shows that public randomness suffices for a weak party to check a strong party. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [20] Jean Tirole. "Hierarchies and Bureaucracies: On the Role of Collusion in Organizations." Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization 2(2):181-214, 1986. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.jleo.a036907

    • Game model: Principal-supervisor-agent with side contracts
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows that a monitor must be paid more for honest reports than the monitored party can offer; relevant to AI monitors that might collude with the policy.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Tirole models a three-tier hierarchy of principal, supervisor and agent in which the supervisor and agent can collude by side contracts. He shows that the principal can restrict attention to collusion-proof contracts, and that the threat of collusion changes optimal incentives and organization design. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [21] Paul Milgrom, John Roberts. "Relying on the Information of Interested Parties." RAND Journal of Economics 17(1):18-32, 1986. https://doi.org/10.2307/2555625

    • Game model: Multi-sender verifiable disclosure
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows that competing interested advocates can substitute for judge sophistication; this is the economic root of AI debate.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    The paper asks when competition among interested parties who supply verifiable information to a decision maker reveals all relevant information. A sophisticated, well-informed decision maker gets full revelation even from one party; a naive or poorly informed one needs competition among parties with sufficiently opposed interests, and even that may not be enough. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [22] Marvin Minsky. "The Society of Mind." Simon & Schuster, New York (book, ISBN 0-671-60740-5), 1986. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Mind

    • Game model: informal society of competing and censoring agents
    • Link to reward hacking: Frames one mind as many agents in conflict, with censor agents that block others, an early informal picture of internal checking that has no incentive analysis.
    • Area: Decomposed agents (internal games between parts)

    Minsky argues that a mind is a society of many simple, mindless agents, and that intelligence emerges from their interactions. The book is a set of short essays on how agencies cooperate, compete and control one another, including censor and suppressor agents that block unwanted mental actions. It gives no formal model or training procedure. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [23] Michael Ben-Or, Shafi Goldwasser, Joe Kilian, Avi Wigderson. "Multi-prover interactive proofs: how to remove intractability assumptions." STOC 1988, 1988. https://doi.org/10.1145/62212.62223

    • Game model: Two isolated provers interrogated separately
    • Link to reward hacking: Separate interrogation of isolated provers makes consistent lying hard, the formal basis for questioning non-communicating AI copies.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    The paper introduces multi-prover interactive proofs, in which a verifier questions two or more provers who cannot communicate with each other during the protocol. It shows that, in this model, perfect zero-knowledge proofs exist for all of NP without any intractability assumption. Separate interrogation replaces cryptographic commitments. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [24] Nick Littlestone. "Learning quickly when irrelevant attributes abound: A new linear-threshold algorithm." Machine Learning 2(4), 1988. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00116827

    • Game model: Online mistake-bound learning; Littlestone dimension; Winnow algorithm
    • Link to reward hacking: Source of the Littlestone dimension, the complexity measure behind mistake bounds in online strategic classification.
    • Area: Strategic ML and truthful elicitation

    Introduces the Winnow algorithm, a multiplicative-update linear-threshold learner for online learning, and proves mistake bounds that grow only logarithmically with the number of irrelevant attributes. The paper also defines the combinatorial dimension (later called the Littlestone dimension) that characterizes optimal mistake bounds in the online model. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [25] John Moore, Rafael Repullo. "Subgame Perfect Implementation." Econometrica 56(5):1191-1220, 1988. https://doi.org/10.2307/1911364

    • Game model: Multi-stage mechanism with challenges
    • Link to reward hacking: The announce-challenge-test structure makes lying a losing move; it maps to AI-vs-AI challenge and cross-examination protocols.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Moore and Repullo characterize choice rules implementable in subgame-perfect equilibrium by multi-stage mechanisms. In many economic environments virtually any choice rule can be implemented, using stages in which one agent can challenge another's announcement and the challenged agent faces a test. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [26] Jacques Cremer, Richard P. McLean. "Full Extraction of the Surplus in Bayesian and Dominant Strategy Auctions." Econometrica 56(6):1247-1257, 1988. https://doi.org/10.2307/1913096

    • Game model: Mechanism with correlated types
    • Link to reward hacking: Shows that correlation among agents' information lets a designer cross-check each report against others and make lying costly; the root of peer prediction.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Cremer and McLean study auctions in which bidders' private information is correlated. They give conditions under which a seller can use lotteries based on other bidders' reports to extract the full surplus, as if values were known, in Bayesian or dominant-strategy auctions. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [27] Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali, Charles Rackoff. "The Knowledge Complexity of Interactive Proof Systems." SIAM Journal on Computing 18(1); conference version STOC 1985, 1989. https://doi.org/10.1137/0218012

    • Game model: Prover-verifier interaction with private coins
    • Link to reward hacking: Defines interactive proofs: a weak verifier is protected from any lying prover by soundness, the base model for AI overseers checking stronger models.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    [28] Robert A. Jacobs, Michael I. Jordan, Steven J. Nowlan, Geoffrey E. Hinton. "Adaptive Mixtures of Local Experts." Neural Computation, 3(1):79-87, 1991. https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1991.3.1.79

    • Game model: experts compete for credit allocated by a gating network
    • Link to reward hacking: Experts compete for the gate's trust and are rewarded for being correct on the cases they win, a competition that rewards accuracy when the training signal is ground truth.
    • Area: Decomposed agents (internal games between parts)

    [29] Moni Naor. "Bit commitment using pseudorandomness." Journal of Cryptology 4(2), 1991. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00196774

    • Game model: Commit-then-reveal
    • Link to reward hacking: Binding commitments stop a model from changing its answer after it sees which part the overseer will check.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    Naor shows how to build a bit commitment scheme from any pseudorandom generator, and hence from any one-way function. The receiver sends a random string, and the committer sends the generator output masked according to the bit. The scheme is statistically binding and computationally hiding. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [30] Bengt Holmstrom, Paul Milgrom. "Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design." Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization 7 (special issue): 24-52, 1991. https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24

    • Game model: multitask principal-agent (moral hazard)
    • Link to reward hacking: The standard economic model of why optimizing a partial, measurable reward diverts effort from unmeasured goals; AI-safety work cites it as the formal root of reward hacking.
    • Area: Principal–agent, contracts and incentive analysis

    The paper extends the principal-agent model to an agent who allocates effort across several tasks, some of which the principal can measure poorly. It shows that strong incentives on measurable tasks pull effort away from unmeasured tasks, so optimal contracts may use low-powered incentives, fixed wages, or restrictions on activities. The model explains features of job design and asset ownership. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [31] László Babai, Lance Fortnow, Carsten Lund. "Non-deterministic exponential time has two-prover interactive protocols." Computational Complexity 1(1), 1991. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01200056

    • Game model: Two-prover protocol with consistency tests
    • Link to reward hacking: Two isolated provers let a weak verifier check exponentially larger claims than one prover, quantifying the value of isolating AI copies.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    The paper proves MIP = NEXP: every language in nondeterministic exponential time has a two-prover interactive protocol with a polynomial-time verifier. The proof uses multilinearity tests and arithmetization so that the verifier can check an exponentially long object through two isolated provers. This shows separate interrogation is strictly more powerful than one prover (under standard assumptions). (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [32] Oded Goldreich, Silvio Micali, Avi Wigderson. "Proofs that yield nothing but their validity or all languages in NP have zero-knowledge proof systems." Journal of the ACM 38(3), 1991. https://doi.org/10.1145/116825.116852

    • Game model: Commit-challenge-response (graph 3-colouring)
    • Link to reward hacking: Proving validity without revealing secrets removes a reason to lie or hide, which supports audits of proprietary AI systems.
    • Area: Honesty mechanisms (games whose equilibrium is truth)

    The paper shows that, assuming one-way functions exist, every language in NP has a zero-knowledge interactive proof, using a commit-and-challenge protocol for graph 3-colouring. It also shows zero-knowledge proofs for graph isomorphism and non-isomorphism without assumptions, and discusses uses in cryptographic protocol design. (summary written by compiler; no author abstract)

    [33] Carsten Lund, Lance Fortnow, Howard Karloff, Noam Nisan. "Algebraic methods for interactive proof systems." Journal of the ACM 39(4), 1992. https://doi.org/10.1145/146585.146605

    • Game model: Sum-check protocol
