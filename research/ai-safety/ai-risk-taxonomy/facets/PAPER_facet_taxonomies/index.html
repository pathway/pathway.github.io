    Seven Facets of AI Safety: Re-sorting Seventeen Classification Schemes into Separate Crosswalks, Mapped to Existing Resources and Rated

    Robin Ranjit Singh Chauhan (with Claude), 2026-11-21, revised 2026-11-22. Working paper.

    Abstract

    Published AI-safety classification schemes do not agree on what they classify. Of seventeen schemes from 2008 to 2026 [1-17], four list causes of harm, three list harm types, four list internal mechanisms, four list research problems and nine list research approaches; two organise by lifecycle. A single merged tree therefore forces unlike things into one hierarchy, and 43 node placements in our earlier twelve-area master tree were recorded as ambiguous. This paper separates the material into seven facets, each answering one question: harms (what harm, to whom), risk sources (what causes it), failure modes (which mechanism breaks), threat models (by what path), dangerous capabilities (what the model can do), safety properties (what we want to be true) and responses (what we do, each tagged control, assessment or enabler). Each facet is a crosswalk from a synthesised tree to the source nodes, quoted in the sources' own words. Facet names and placements were checked against 195 verbatim definitions from 38 risk-management, safety-engineering, security and AI-safety sources; the check renamed facets, moved node placements and removed seven search-found nodes that were thresholds, findings or frameworks rather than members of their facet. The 462 distinct corpus nodes place into the facets with 28 left over: structural containers, attribute axes and lab lists. Seven literature searches, one per facet, then attached 245 source entries (174 distinct sources) to the facet trees, with citation counts from Semantic Scholar, and one rule tiers every source as central, important or peripheral. Two further layers sit on the facets. The external layer maps eleven existing resources (983 items) onto the facet nodes, with 1236 mappings and 685 datapoints; 114 of the 161 nodes receive a mapping. It lists what the resources cover and what they miss. The ratings layer rates 26 threat-model scenarios and 43 response sub-areas on seven anchored dimensions, with three language-model raters, a written justification for every rating, and rankings that never multiply ordinal levels. Ratings and external gaps then changed the taxonomy in four ways (misuse and accident scenarios restored, scenarios stated as trigger, path and end state, counter-models moved out of the tree, six response sub-areas split), and the ratings were repeated. All mappings, tiers and ratings are judgement by language models. No human expert has reviewed the facets or rated anything.

    1. Why facets

    A taxonomy is a classification along one axis. The seventeen schemes we collected do not share an axis, and few are taxonomies in the strict sense of an exhaustive partition: most are problem lists, approach lists or the chapter structure of a report. We call them classification schemes, and use the sources' own word "taxonomy" only where they do. Hendrycks, Mazeika and Woodside split catastrophic risk by source: malicious use, AI race, organisational risks, rogue AIs [8]. Weidinger et al. split language-model risk by harm type: discrimination, information hazards, misinformation, malicious use, human-computer interaction harms, automation and environmental harms [6]. Manheim and Garrabrant split one mechanism, proxy failure, into regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial Goodhart [4]. Leech et al. split the field by what the researcher does: 96 nodes under eight approach headings [14]. Ji et al. organise by lifecycle, forward and backward alignment, and add a goal list, RICE [9].

    Our earlier synthesis forced all of these into one twelve-area tree with a problem side (areas 1 to 6) and a response side (areas 7 to 12). It worked as an index, but section 6 of that crosswalk lists 43 placements that needed a paragraph of reasoning each, because a harm-type node and a mechanism node were being asked to share a sub-area. The cleanest repair is to stop merging across axes: give each axis its own tree, and connect the trees with matrices.

    Two more layers answer a different need. A reader of a taxonomy also asks what other people already know about each node, and which nodes matter most. The external layer (section 3.5) maps eleven existing assessments, such as the MIT AI Risk Repository, an expert Delphi study, incident databases and deployment trackers, onto our nodes. It also records what they have that we lack. The ratings layer (section 3.6) rates scenarios and response sub-areas with written reasons and shows the disagreement between raters. The ratings then fed back into the taxonomy and the facets changed (section 3.7). This paper describes the facets, both layers and the revision loop. It says plainly where a result is judgement by language models. No human expert has reviewed the facets, the mappings or the ratings.

    2. Corpus

    The corpus is the 17 schemes in Table 1, extracted node by node into taxonomies/tx_ext_*.md with the source's own node names, then attached to the master tree in unified_taxonomy.md. The parser (parse_unified.py) recovers 479 node records and 462 distinct (source, node) pairs. Table 1 sums to 462. An earlier version of this paper and the master file give 472. That figure counts the nine values of the Repository's three attribute axes [11] and one node name that occurs twice in Shah et al. [13]; the parser reads the axis names only and merges the repeat. We use 462 throughout.

    3. Method

    3.1 Choosing the facets

    Before naming facets we collected the standard vocabulary: 195 verbatim definitions of 102 terms from 38 risk-management, safety-engineering, security and AI-safety sources, with an analysis of where they conflict (vocabulary/analysis.md). Four findings shaped the facets. Risk has three definitions in use, likelihood times severity in the regulatory texts [68] [69], effect of uncertainty on objectives in ISO 31000 [70], and a set of (scenario, likelihood, consequence) triplets in Kaplan and Garrick [71]; we adopt the third, so a risk is a row across facets and not a facet. A hazard is a source of harm in most standards but a system state in Leveson [72], which matters for whether a misaligned model is a source or a state. The AI-safety sense of threat model, one path from cause to catastrophe, is the standards' scenario or loss scenario [71] [72], not the security sense of a whole-system analysis [73]. Every standard definition of mitigation, control and barrier requires the measure to modify risk [74] [75] [76]; interpretability and agent-foundations research do not, so the response-side facet is named responses and each of its nodes is tagged control (modifies risk, ISO 31000 [76]), assessment (risk identification, analysis and evaluation [70] [75]) or enabler (work that makes a control possible but does not itself modify risk; no standard term).

    We started from the three facets most people name, risks, failure modes and mitigations, renamed the first to harms and the third to responses for the reasons above, and tested candidates against two criteria: the facet must answer one question that no other facet answers, and at least one source scheme, or a well-known body of work, must cut along it. Seven passed.

    Facet One-question test Main corpus sources

    Four candidates were rejected as separate trees. Risk factors (race dynamics, opacity, scale) are drivers of risk sources and are kept as attributes. Detection and evidence, which eval reveals which failure, is a matrix between failure modes and responses, not a tree. Worldview cruxes (timelines, takeoff speed) explain disagreements and belong in the disagreements file. Maturity and actor (demonstrated vs theoretical, lab vs state) are attributes on response nodes.

    3.2 Placing corpus nodes

    Each facet has a tree of 4 to 8 top nodes, written by us. Placement rules in facet_spec.json pull corpus nodes either by master sub-area code (for example, failure-mode node F1 "Specification failures" takes everything in master 3.0 to 3.3) or by an explicit (source, substring) pick (for example, risk-source node S2 "Misalignment" takes Shah's "Misalignment" [13], Hendrycks's "Rogue AIs" [8] and the Repository's "7.1 AI pursuing its own goals" [11]). A node may land in two facets when its name serves both: "Rogue AIs" is a risk source and a loss-of-control harm. The builder reports any corpus node claimed by no facet.

    3.3 Literature search and citation tiers

    Two rounds of search attached literature to the facet trees. In the first round, threat models, capabilities and safety properties had one Sonnet search agent each. Each agent was given the one-question test, the corpus seed nodes, about twenty candidate documents and a rule: verify every arXiv identifier with the arXiv API and every other URL by fetching the page, and drop anything unverifiable. They returned 21, 20 and 21 verified sources. The first-round files are kept as _sources/*.v1.json.

    In the second round all seven facets got an agent with the same brief (_sources/BRIEF.md), the facet tree, the first-round file where one existed, and the top-100 list from our all-time core-paper ranking as an anchor. Each agent built a literature tree whose top nodes are the facet's own codes, gave every leaf at least one citation with a role (defines, demonstrates, surveys, example), proposed refinements to the facet, and tiered its sources. The agents returned 245 source entries (33 harms, 31 risk sources, 40 failure modes, 43 threat models, 24 capabilities, 34 safety properties, 40 responses) and literature trees of 13 to 27 leaves per facet. The 245 entries are 174 distinct sources, counted by arXiv identifier or else URL; 51 of the 174 appear in two or more facets. The leaves carry 314 citations in all. The threat-model file is the revised one of 2026-11-22 (section 3.7); the second-round file of 28 sources is kept as _sources/threat_models.v2.json. Citation counts were filled from the Semantic Scholar batch and title-search endpoints.

    The agents' own tiers were inconsistent: tier-1 shares ran from 29 to 73 percent. We therefore keep the agents' tiers in the data as a record but assign the published tier by one rule in the builder. A source scores 2 if some leaf cites it with the role defines, plus 1 if it is on the core-paper list, plus 1 if it has 300 or more citations, plus 0.5 if two or more leaves cite it. Tier 1 (central) is a score of 2.5 or more: the source defines a node's concept and shows at least one sign of uptake. Tier 3 (peripheral) is a source no leaf cites, or one with score 0 and under 100 citations. Everything else is tier 2. The rule is printed in every crosswalk header, and the bibliography shows the agent's tier where it differs. The thresholds are our choice. They are not derived from data.

    3.4 Checking names and placements against the standard vocabulary

    After the first build we audited every facet name and tree node against the definitions in vocabulary/terms.md. The rule was the one-question test read literally: a node stays only if the standard definition of the facet's term fits it. The audit changed the following; each crosswalk prints its own placement notes. The earlier spec was not kept, so we cannot count how many placements moved.

    • Mitigations became responses, with a control, assessment or enabler tag on each tree node (section 3.1). Interpretability, theory and contributing disciplines are enablers; evaluation and safety cases are assessment; training methods, runtime control, safer design, multi-agent tools and governance are controls. Training-side controls are prevention barriers and runtime controls are recovery barriers in bow-tie terms [75].
    • Harms node H1 was "Catastrophic misuse harms". Misuse names who acts, not what is harmed, so H1 is now "Mass-casualty and critical-infrastructure harms (CBRN, cyber)" and the malicious-use headers, agents released with harmful goals and open-weight tampering (master 1.0, 1.5, 1.6) moved to risk source S1. Loss of control (H6) is kept but marked as a top event [75], not a harm.
    • The Repository's Entity, Intent and Timing axes [11] left risk sources: they are attributes of a source (human or AI, intentional or not, pre- or post-deployment), not sources, since a risk source is an element [70]. "Mistakes and accidents" split into model mistakes (S3) and organisational failure (S4), following Leveson's placement of the organisation in the control structure [72].
    • Failure modes: "Situational awareness" [7] is a capability, not a failure, and moved to capabilities; Carlsmith's containers (training saints, model classes, prerequisites) left the facet. F2 is now "Misspecified or contested values" (pluralism is a fact, not a failure); F4 is "Susceptibility to adversarial input and tampering" (a failure mode names the manner of failure [77], not the attack); F8 drops "novel".
    • Shah's alignment targets for groups (master 6.2) moved from responses to safety properties: they name what the system should satisfy. Shallow Review's lab list (master 12.5) is a directory of actors and left the facets.
    • Seven search-found nodes were dropped (drop_sourced in facet_spec.json has seven entries): a CBRN capability threshold and a RAND evaluation finding (a threshold and a finding, not capabilities); MACHIAVELLI's power-seeking behaviour [36] and Sleeper Agents' persistent deception [44] (a propensity and a training finding); Shah's mistake and structural taxonomy and a safety-case structuring framework (a classification and a method, not scenarios); and "assurance and robustness" as a property (assurance is an argument that a property holds, a safety case [74] [78]). None of the seven appears in the current sourced trees, so the entries now act as a record.

    The second-round search agents proposed 24 refinements. We applied those the one-question test and the standard terms support, and recorded the rest as held in facet_spec.json notes:

    • Harms H6 was "Loss of control". Loss of control is an event; the harm class behind it is existential or irreversible catastrophe, which Bostrom defines by consequence [79], and H6 is renamed to that with loss of control named as its top event [75]. H4 becomes "Economic, organisational and environmental harms", since NIST AI RMF lists harm to organisations as its own class [69].
    • Threat models: the top "Misuse and organizational failure" was dropped on 2026-11-21, because its leaves named risk sources (S1, S4), not paths from failure to harm. The rating pass showed that this left no scenario for several harms, and the top came back on 2026-11-22 as "Misuse and accidents" with specific scenarios (section 3.7). The agent's new leaves for Kenton's consensus threat model and Critch's multipolar failure are kept. Drexler's comprehensive AI services moved out of the tree as a counter-model.
    • Capabilities: "CBRN uplift" is renamed "Biological and chemical weapons uplift" because no radiological or nuclear source was found, and "Situational awareness, sandbagging and scheming skills" becomes "Evaluation-evasion and oversight-subversion skills", keeping it skill-only after Shevlane's capability versus propensity split [38].
    • Safety properties gains a top node "Alignment target (to whom, which values)", matching the corpus nodes moved from master 6.2, with Gabriel [63], Sorensen, Kirk and Huang as sources.
    • Risk sources: a note records that the five-way split is not exhaustive and that gradual disempowerment sits under S5 as a structural risk.
    • Held for a later pass because they need the agents' trees extended: a sandbagging leaf under F5, an emergent-misalignment leaf under F3, a task-time-horizon leaf under autonomy, a weight-security node under M4 (the split part 10.5a of section 3.7 covers weight security in the ratings only), a re-sort of H3 on Shelby's themes.

    3.5 Mapping existing resources

    The facets describe what a node is. Other groups already hold data about many of these nodes. We mapped eleven existing resources onto the facet nodes to find out what is known and what is missing. All mappings are judgement by language models. No human has checked them.

    Resources. The eleven are listed in Table 4. They are the MIT AI Risk Repository [11] and the MIT Delphi study of 272 experts [86]; the MIT mitigation taxonomy [87] together with Uuk et al. [88]; the AI Safety Agendas map [91]; the AIR 2024 risk categories [89]; the OECD AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor with the AI Incident Database [92]; measured capability status from METR, the UK AI Security Institute, Epoch and the frontier safety policies [93]; the forecasts of the XPT tournament [94] and of Grace et al. [95]; deployment status from the FLI AI Safety Index [96], SaferAI [97], AI Lab Watch [98] and system cards; the priority judgements of Open Philanthropy [99], 80,000 Hours [100], Casper et al. [83], Anwar et al. [10], Hendrycks et al. [5] and the Shallow Review [101]; and the FCAIR-O ontology [90]. We chose them because each holds either a taxonomy that overlaps ours or a datapoint (a rating, a probability, a maturity tier, a head-count, a measurement) that bears on one of our nodes. The choice is ours. Metaculus was not obtained.

    How mapped. A node index (external/NODES.json) lists every facet node that an item may map to: 161 nodes in all (6 harms, 5 risk sources, 8 failure modes, 31 threat-model nodes, 25 capability nodes, 29 property nodes and 57 response nodes). One Sonnet agent per resource read the resource, with a brief (external/BRIEF.md) that said: open every page or file you cite, drop what you cannot open, and never invent an identifier, a number or a quote. Each agent wrote one file with four parts: the resource's own items; map rows from an item to a node, each with a relation; evidence rows, each a datapoint with a dimension, a verbatim quote and a URL; and the items that fit no node, with a reason. Fit is judged against the one-question test of each facet.

    Relation vocabulary. A map row has one of four relations. Equivalent: same concept. Narrower: the external item is part of our node. Broader: our node is part of the external item. Related: they overlap and neither contains the other. Of the 1236 map rows, 805 are related, 195 narrower, 182 equivalent and 54 broader. A datapoint has one of nine dimensions: severity, likelihood, maturity, difficulty, neglectedness, scales to superhuman, precursor, capability status and coverage. The last three are evidence kinds, not rating dimensions. Of the 685 evidence rows, 161 are coverage, 112 likelihood, 109 severity, 92 maturity, 87 neglectedness, 65 difficulty, 30 capability status and 29 precursor. No row concerns scaling to superhuman systems.

    Validation. Code checks every row. A map row stays only if its node is in the node index, its relation is in the vocabulary and its item exists. Three unit tests cover the checks. No script re-opens the URLs, so the quotes are as good as the agents' reading. The mapping layer of this paper rests on that reading.

    Counts and coverage. The map rows reach 114 of the 161 nodes. Section 4.6 gives the coverage per facet. In short, every harm, risk source and failure mode has at least one external map. Safety properties have the weakest coverage: 7 of 29 nodes.

    What the resources say we lack. Items that fit no node are our gaps. Of the 983 items, 264 are unmapped, and two resources hold 207 of these (incidents 101, FCAIR-O 106), mostly record classes and attribute values. The substantive gaps are listed in section 4.6.

    3.6 Rating and ranking

    We rate some nodes so that a reader can see which scenarios look most severe and likely and which responses look mature, tractable or neglected. The ratings are judgement made inspectable. They are not measurement. The International AI Safety Report states that no validated method exists for rating the severity or likelihood of AI harms [85]. The closest published precedent is the table of Hendrycks et al., which gives twelve problems one to three dots on three factors with written text per level, and which its authors call a rough sketch [5].

    Units. Likelihood and consequence belong to a scenario, one line of Kaplan and Garrick's set of triplets [71]. A cause category or a mechanism has no event to which a probability attaches. We therefore rate the 26 threat-model scenarios on the risk side. Harms, risk sources and failure modes are not rated. They inherit through a categorical link table (ratings/links.json) from scenario to harm, risk-source and failure-mode codes, so a reader sees which rated scenarios sit behind each category. On the response side we rate sub-areas, not the eight M nodes, because one M node mixes deployed practice with lab research (M3 holds RLHF and scalable oversight). There are 43 response units: 31 whole sub-areas and 12 parts of the six split sub-areas (section 3.7). Capabilities and safety properties are not rated. The field reports measured capability status, not judged proximity, and that is a separate task.

    Dimensions and anchored levels. Every level has anchor text that defines it. The text was written before any rating. Verbal levels without anchors are read differently by different readers.

    Dimension Applies to Levels
    Severity scenario S1 endurable harm at any scope, or crushing harm confined to a local or regional population; S2 crushing harm to a global population, recoverable within generations; S3 crushing and trans-generational (extinction, permanent loss of control, permanent curtailment of potential). Built from Bostrom's scope and severity grid [79]. Irreversibility sits inside S3.
    Likelihood scenario Probability that the scenario occurs at the rated severity or worse by end of 2036: L0 under 1 percent; L1 1 to 10; L2 10 to 50; L3 over 50. "No defensible estimate" and "not applicable" are allowed.
    Maturity response R0 argument or theory only; R1 shown in toy, small or model-organism settings; R2 published at frontier scale, in research; R3 deployed at one or more frontier developers and documented publicly.
    Difficulty response D1 incremental: progress is expected inside the current approach; D2 fundamental: needs a new approach or rests on an unsolved conceptual problem. After Casper et al. [83].
    Binding obstacle response One tag: technical, coordination, governance, economic or none.
    Neglectedness response Full-time-equivalent people worldwide: N1 over 100; N2 10 to 100; N3 under 10 (higher means more neglected); "unknown" is allowed.
    Scales to superhuman 19 control responses (M3, M4, M5) One tag with a quote: argued yes, argued no, contested or unknown. A flag, not a score, because no comparison of oversight methods exists.

    A further flag, precursor observed, is derived and not rated: it is true when a literature leaf for the scenario has a citation with the role demonstrates. Scales are short because field practice for judged items uses two to three levels. Larger scales appear where organisations are graded against checklists [96] [97].

    Justification record. Each rating by each rater carries the value, a one-sentence rationale that states why the anchor text applies, one or two evidence items (a source, a verbatim quote and a role), a confidence label with reasons, the rater and the date. A row with a missing field counts as unrated. The builder checks every quote against the named source pool. We did not use a full safety case per rating. Safety cases are per-system deployment documents [78], and field practice for ratings is a value plus a cited reason.

    Confidence. We adapted GRADE [84]. GRADE applies four labels to a body of evidence: high, moderate, low and very low. In our version a rater starts at high only if the evidence directly measures the rated quantity for this node, and at low otherwise. The rater moves down one level for each of indirectness (proxy evidence), imprecision (the value sits between two anchors) and self-assessment (a developer describes its own practice). The rater moves up one level for corroboration by an independent author group. The aggregate confidence is the median label, lowered one level when the raters' values span two or more levels. We call this GRADE-adapted because it is not the GRADE procedure.

    Raters and aggregation. Three raters rate blind with the same anchors and the same unit order: Claude Sonnet, Claude Opus and Claude Haiku. They are one vendor family, and their errors are likely correlated. The aggregate of an ordinal dimension is the lower median, sorted(values)[(n-1)//2]. The median resists the compression that a mean suffers [102]. Non-values (no estimate, not applicable, unknown) are left out of the median; if two or more raters give a non-value, the non-value wins. For a tag dimension the aggregate is the plurality value, and a tie is reported as contested. The builder reports the range across raters, the exact-agreement rate and the mean span per dimension. A dimension with exact agreement under one half is marked contested. Weights are equal, because performance weighting needs seed questions with known answers, and none exist for ten-year AI scenarios [103].

    Rankings. No ranking multiplies ordinal levels. The product of ordinals is the documented failure of the failure-mode risk priority number and of risk matrices. Bowles lists four flaws of the priority number: ordinal ranks used as numbers, a scale without continuity, equal numbers for very different cases, and varying sensitivity [81]. Cox shows that risk matrices can compare only a small fraction of hazard pairs, can assign higher ratings to smaller risks, and do worse than random when frequency and severity are negatively correlated [80]. Krisper adds that on ordinal scales any combining rule is a convention [82]. A negative correlation of severity and likelihood is plausible here, since the worst harms are the least likely. We use three outputs instead.

    • Risk fronts. Pareto fronts on (severity, likelihood) over the median ratings. A scenario is dominated only if another is at least as high on both and higher on one. Front 1 holds the undominated scenarios. Fronts are also computed for each rater. A scenario on front 1 for every rater is robust. A scenario on front 1 for the median only is contested. Inside a front the display order is severity first; this is a display convention and not a ranking.
    • Response table. Maturity, difficulty, binding obstacle and neglectedness per sub-area, grouped by M node, with two set operations: neglected and tractable (N3 and D1) and mature (R3).
    • Gap list. For each failure mode linked from a front-1 scenario, the primary responses from the failure-mode by response matrix with their maturity. A gap is a failure mode none of whose primary responses reaches R2. A split sub-area counts at its most mature part.

    Consult-first rule. Each rater brief names, for each dimension, which external datapoints to read before other evidence: for severity the Delphi study and the incident data; for likelihood the Delphi study and the forecasts; for maturity the deployment status, the agendas map and the mitigation resources; for difficulty and neglectedness the priority judgements and the mitigation resources; for scaling the literature. A rater who departs from the external datapoints must say why. A rater must state the horizon and scale mismatch of a datapoint as an indirectness downgrade. The Delphi horizon is about 2030 and its severity scale has five levels. Our horizon is 2036 and our scale has three.

    The honest reading has four parts.

    • External evidence alone did not raise agreement. From run 1 to run 2, agreement fell for severity (89 to 72), likelihood (39 to 28), maturity (27 to 22) and scaling (50 to 12), and rose for difficulty (62 to 78) and neglectedness (27 to 43). Traceability rose: 575 of 832 evidence items in run 2 cite an external datapoint, against none in run 1, and 542 of those 575 quotes are verbatim in the evidence pool. We think, from the rationales, that the Delphi and forecast data have another horizon and another scale than ours, and that raters handled the mismatch in different ways. This is a judgement. We did not measure it.
    • Sharper scenario definitions raised agreement. In run 3, where every scenario states trigger, path and end state, agreement is 100 percent on severity and 65 percent on likelihood. Run 3 changed the units, the brief, the evidence and the taxonomy at once, so the table shows what changed and not why. Because the severity of an end state is partly set by the anchor text, we trust the likelihood rise more than the severity rise.
    • Maturity stays low at 28 percent, although we split six sub-areas. Three causes remain (section 3.7): one rater reads deployment evidence differently, the line between R1 and R2 is unclear, and some sub-areas have no maturity datapoint.
    • No run repeated an earlier brief. The noise of model raters is therefore not separated from the effect of the evidence. Run 3 also mixes rows from different times: the scenario and split rows are run 3, and the rows for the whole sub-areas are run 2, with the same brief and the old units.

    5. Observations

    1. The cut predicts the era. Mechanism and problem-list taxonomies cluster in 2008 to 2021; risk-source and harm-type lists in 2021 to 2026; approach lists in 2023 to 2025. The field moved from naming problems to organising responses, and the newest documents [15] [17] organise by lifecycle or by who must act.
    2. Properties are under-taxonomised. Only Ji's RICE [9] states goals as a list. The property vocabulary exists [53] [54] [48] but lives in individual papers, not in surveys, so a reader of the 2025 approach lists never sees what the approaches are for. The external layer shows the same gap: seven of 29 property nodes have any external map (section 4.6).
    3. Threat models are forum literature. Ten of the 43 threat-model sources are posts on LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, GreaterWrong or a personal blog (counted by domain). They are the field's shared stories, yet no peer-reviewed taxonomy collects them; Hadshar's evidence review [67] is the closest.
    4. Capabilities are set by labs. The capability thresholds that structure evaluation come from four frontier-lab or industry documents [32] [45] [46] [47]; academic work measures against categories the labs chose.
    5. Two facets carry most of the ambiguity. The 43 ambiguous placements in the single master tree were mostly nodes that were a risk source in one reading and a failure mode or harm in another. Separate facets with a one-question test each remove the need to choose; the node is listed in both and the overlap is visible.
    6. Misuse and accident scenarios fill much of front 1. Six of the 11 scenarios on front 1 are misuse or accident scenarios. Takeover scenarios reach front 1 only at S3 with L1 (T1.2, T1.3, T1.8). The aggregate likelihood is L0 for 11 of the 26 scenarios. Among the eight takeover scenarios of T1, four are L0 (T1.1, T1.4, T1.5, T1.6) and four are L1 (T1.2, T1.3, T1.7, T1.8). Rating by one end-of-2036 horizon, and the S1 rule for bounded harms, shape this result (section 4.7). Before the revision of section 3.7, 17 of 18 scenarios were S3 and the ratings could not show this contrast.
    7. One gap in the response list. Incorrigibility and shutdown resistance (F7) is the only failure mode on a front-1 path with no primary response at frontier scale. It rests on one scenario with one source.
    8. Maturity is the dimension raters disagree on most. Exact agreement is 28 percent after the split. Deployment status is per developer and per product, while our units are sub-areas.
    9. The prior-art search found no source that does the same. See section 5.1.

    5.1 Position against prior art

    One agent searched for multi-facet AI-safety taxonomies that give definitions, a crosswalk and ratings of nodes (_sources/ratings_research/PRIOR_ART.md). It opened each source by fetch, download or text extraction. Where it read only an abstract, the file says so. It found 31 works.

    What exists. The closest on structure is FCAIR-O [90], a 2026 preprint with eight facets (causal sources, vulnerabilities, failure modes, harms, properties, controls, evidence and legal roles), 74 causal leaves and 221 concept identifiers. The agent found no ratings in its abstract. The closest on ratings of taxonomy nodes are the MIT Delphi study [86], which rates 24 risks on severity and probability with 272 experts, and the AI Safety Agendas map [91], which gives 58 agendas against 12 problems an evidence-maturity tier per cell. On responses, the MIT mitigation taxonomy [87] has four categories and 23 subcategories with counts only, and Uuk et al. [88] have 27 mitigations rated by 76 experts. The Risk Repository [11] has the largest crosswalk (74 frameworks) with two facets, cause and domain. AIR 2024 [89] has 314 risk categories from 24 policies. Hendrycks et al. [5] rate twelve problems with written anchors and no multiplication.

    What is already done by others. A causal by domain split of risk [11]. Severity and probability ratings of risk nodes by experts [86]. A problem by agenda maturity grid [91]. A separation of causes, harms and controls [90].

    What this work adds. Seven facets joined by the Kaplan and Garrick triplet [71]. A failure-mode by response matrix. A one-question placement test for every node, checked against 195 standard definitions. A crosswalk of 17 schemes. A tier rule for literature. Ratings with a written justification, an evidence quote and a confidence label for each value, and rankings that avoid products of ordinals. A feedback loop from ratings to the taxonomy, with the effect measured in agreement between raters.

    Caveats. The search was one agent's and the result "no source found" is not proof. For several works the finding "no ratings" rests on an abstract only. The agent did not open the FCAIR-O full text. The mapping agent later read its codebook, which has 221 concept identifiers, and the 61 evidence rows we took from it are all coverage rows, so we found no ratings in it. The claim of novelty is for the combination, and it is weak on each part.

    6. Limits

    • The seven facet trees, the placement rules, the failure-mode by response matrix and the control/assessment/enabler tags are our synthesis. No human expert has reviewed them, and no human has reviewed the mappings or the ratings. The standards define control and risk assessment; enabler is our word for the remainder.
    • Corpus quotes are the source's own node names as recorded in the extraction files; nine nodes are marked as taken from notes rather than fetched text.
    • Search-agent quotes are mostly title phrases or page summaries, not body text; some web-post years are from memory. Each source's existence was verified, but not every attributed claim. Roles (defines, demonstrates, surveys, example) are the agents' judgement and drive the tier rule; a wrong role moves a source one tier.
    • Citation counts are Semantic Scholar's on 2026-11-21 and favour older papers; the 300-citation term in the tier rule therefore under-ranks 2024 to 2025 work. Books and posts matched by title search may carry the count of a different edition. The tier thresholds are our choice.
    • The corpus under-represents fairness, security and HCI taxonomies, and policy frameworks other than the three the capabilities search added. We did not record the rule by which we chose the 17 schemes.
    • The vocabulary check (section 3.4) used ISO 31000 wording at second hand (via the EU Agency for Railways and the NIST glossary) and FMEA wording via Wikipedia [77]; ISO/IEC 23894 and IEC 60812 were not read.
    • Counts depend on the parser. The 462 distinct nodes differ from the 472 of the master file for the reason in section 2. The drop_sourced entries in the spec are a record only, since none matches the current trees.
    • The raters are three models of one vendor. Their errors are likely correlated. No human rated, and no model from a second vendor rated. Agreement among the three raters is not evidence of correctness.
    • Confidence is mostly very low. The aggregate confidence is very low for 21 of 26 scenario rows and for 29 of 43 response rows. The likelihood of a scenario by 2036 has few direct datapoints. The lowest band L0 is dominated by uncertainty about the estimating model, not by inputs.
    • Horizon and scale mismatch. The Delphi study uses a five-year horizon (to about 2030) and a five-level severity scale. The XPT forecasts give 2030, 2050 and 2100. Our horizon is 2036 and our severity scale has three levels. We did not interpolate. Raters were told to state the mismatch, and they handled it in different ways. Metaculus was not obtained. Incident and deployment data are dated snapshots (OECD monitor 2026-11-22 with 19,273 records; AI Incident Database snapshot of 2026-11-16) and will change.
    • The mappings are agent judgement. The relation and the node of each map row come from one Sonnet agent per resource. No person checked a sample. No script re-opens the resource URLs. Some resources were mapped at subdomain level, not per record. Of the quotes raters gave as evidence, 629 of 667 external quotes and 76 of 93 response-literature quotes and 48 of 130 threat-model-literature quotes were found verbatim in their named pool. Of the quotes raters took from pages they opened themselves, none of 45 could be checked this way.
    • The split assignments are unreviewed. The agent assigned corpus nodes to the parts by node name. It asks for review of 7.5, 10.5 and 12.1. The split rule is ours, and our own first list of candidates was partly wrong (section 3.7).
    • Maturity and scaling stay weak. Maturity (28 percent) and scaling to superhuman systems (16 percent) have exact agreement under one half. The R1 and R2 anchor line is unclear. Some sub-areas have no maturity datapoint. The scaling dimension has no external datapoint at all.
    • The runs are not controlled. No run repeated an earlier brief. Run 3 mixes rows from three times. Unit order was the same for every rater, and shuffling was planned and not done. In run 1, three rater files were re-run after model refusals. Three rows in run 3 were rejected as incomplete.
    • The prior-art search was one agent's. See section 5.1.

    7. Reproduce

    Every script is in the facets folder, and the recipe is in facet_construct_recipie/ (start with its README.md; one file per step, with the schemas, a glossary and the agent prompts in agents/).

    bash facet_construct_recipie/rebuild.sh rebuilds all scripted outputs in place, in dependency order. bash facet_construct_recipie/rebuild.sh --check copies the folder to a scratch directory, rebuilds there and compares 21 generated files with the files on disk, ignoring date lines. These are the seven crosswalk.md files and README.md, the vocabulary files biblio.md and terms.md, the external files NODES.json, CROSSWALK.md, VALIDATION.md and evidence_pool.json, the four rating unit files, sources.json, RATINGS.md and JUSTIFICATIONS.md. On 2026-11-22 the check ended with "all generated files reproduce". It also runs the parser, the external tests (3), the rating tests (12) and paper_tier1.py. The last prints sections 4.5 and Appendix A to a scratch file and does not splice them; the splice into this paper is done by hand and was compared separately.

    The check does not cover the inputs that a person or an agent produced. The authored inputs are the corpus extraction files, the master tree, facet_spec.json, vocabulary/analysis.md, ratings/anchors.json, ratings/links.json, the rater brief, the design file, and the prose of this paper. The agent-produced inputs are the vocabulary definitions, the seven literature files in _sources/, the four ratings-research files, the eleven resource files in external/, the revised threat-model and split files, and every rating row in ratings/raw*/. To reproduce these, launch the agents again from facet_construct_recipie/agents/. The results will differ. Citation-count filling and corpus extraction are not scripted. The archived pages RATINGS_v1.md and JUSTIFICATIONS_v1.md have no generator.

    To change a facet: edit facet_spec.json or the facet's _sources file, keep the old file as a versioned copy, never reuse a retired code, update ratings/links.json for new scenario codes, run rebuild.sh, re-map the external resources onto new codes and re-rate the changed units. python3 paper_tier1.py regenerates section 4.5 and Appendix A of this paper from the same files.
